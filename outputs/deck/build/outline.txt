 1. [-           ] Tighter financing goes with lower ESG scores in Taiwan’s food industry
 2. [-           ] Higher financial constraints go with lower ESG scores, supporting H1, but only as a between-firm pattern tied to firm size
 3. [Introduction] ESG research asks whether ESG pays; whether a firm’s finances shape its ESG is much less studied
 4. [Introduction] Two theories predict opposite signs for the same coefficient, so the data must decide
 5. [Methods     ] The panel keeps 219 of 250 rows: 22 are 2015 lag inputs and only 9 fail the sample rules
 6. [Methods     ] The outcome is the TESG score and constraints are measured by the SA index, built from size and age
 7. [Methods     ] The model follows the Hausman rule, uses firm-clustered errors and is stress-tested four ways
 8. [Results     ] Most variation lies between firms: 83% of TESG and 96% of SA variance
 9. [Results     ] The link lives between firms: r = −0.39 across firm means, +0.02 once firm and year effects are removed
10. [Results     ] Tests do not reject random effects, and the fixed-effects SA estimate is three times less precise
11. [Results     ] A one-unit rise in the SA index goes with a 4.8-point lower TESG score (p = 0.0498)
12. [Results     ] The sign holds in all four checks; only the main model (5%) and the lagged index (10%) are significant
13. [Results     ] The association sits in the social pillar (−8.27, p = 0.002); E and G are not significant
14. [Discussion  ] The result fits the discretionary-cost view and repeats the size sensitivity seen in earlier Taiwan work
15. [Discussion  ] Read it as an association between firms, not as a causal effect of constraints on ESG
16. [Discussion  ] Constrained firms score lower on ESG, so low scores may partly reflect tight finance
